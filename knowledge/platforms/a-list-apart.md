## Trigger Context

Triggered by mentions of 'A List Apart', 'ALA', or 'web design publication'

## Language

The primary language for A List Apart is **English (US)** . All articles must be written in American English, using professional, formal, yet accessible vocabulary. The publication serves a global audience of senior web professionals, so clarity and precision are paramount. Non-native English submissions are accepted if they meet the editorial bar, but they must be proofread by a native speaker before submission. Avoid region-specific idioms, slang, or colloquialisms that may not translate.

## Good Fit

- Thought leadership pieces: Articles that propose a new methodology, challenge an industry norm, or synthesize existing ideas into a novel framework (e.g., "A Dao of Web Design," "Responsive Web Design").
- Deeply researched design critiques: Projects involving rigorous analysis of user behavior, accessibility standards, or design systems with data-backed conclusions.
- Ethics and inclusivity-focused tools: Libraries, frameworks, or design patterns that prioritize accessibility, privacy, or ethical data handling.
- Long-form technical philosophy: Projects where the code or design is secondary to the underlying principles—e.g., a CSS methodology that argues for a specific mental model.
- Historical or retrospective analysis: Projects that trace the evolution of a web technology or design trend, offering lessons for current practitioners.

## Poor Fit

- Quick tutorials or "how-to" lists: Step-by-step guides for beginners (e.g., "How to Center a Div") belong on CSS-Tricks or freeCodeCamp.
- Marketing or promotional content: Articles that primarily exist to promote a commercial product, service, or conference.
- Opinion pieces without evidence: Hot takes, rants, or personal anecdotes lacking citations, data, or case studies.
- Tool comparisons or reviews: Articles like "React vs. Vue in 2024" are too reductive for ALA's depth.
- Code-heavy walkthroughs: Submissions where the core value is a code block rather than a conceptual argument.

## Format And Style Notes

- Open with a provocative or declarative thesis statement. Do not "warm up" the reader with greetings or context. State your argument in the first two paragraphs.
- Use subheadings to structure a logical argument, not a list of topics. Each H2 should advance the thesis, not change the subject.
- Cite authoritative sources and real-world examples. Every claim should be backed by a link to a study, a quote from a recognized expert, or a case study from a known website.
- Write quotable one-liners that can serve as pull quotes. Editors will highlight these. Craft sentences that are self-contained and memorable.
- Avoid first-person plural ("we") unless it refers to the entire web industry. Do not use "we" to mean "the author and the reader." Use "I" for personal observations or "the industry" for collective actions.
- Use code snippets sparingly and only to illustrate a concept, not to teach syntax. Code should be a *visual aid*, not the main content. Keep snippets under 10 lines.
- End with a call to action that is intellectual, not transactional. Invite readers to reconsider their practices or join a conversation, not to download something or sign up.
- Maintain a formal but accessible tone. Use precise vocabulary, but avoid jargon that hasn't been defined. Write for a senior audience, but assume they are reading on a mobile device during a commute.

## Tone

Write as a respected colleague giving a keynote: authoritative, evidence-based, and always pushing the reader to think deeper.
